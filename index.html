BUILT-IN STUDY CONTENT

Laboratory Identification Guide

These learning notes are part of this website. They are general principles; the correct test panel depends on the suspected organism, sample and veterinary laboratory protocol.

1. Gram staining

Gram-positive: purple/violet appearance due to retention of crystal violet–iodine complex.

Gram-negative: pink/red appearance after counterstaining.

Old cultures, damaged cells, thick smears and staining errors can produce variable results.

2. Morphology and arrangement

Cocci: spherical cells; may occur in clusters, chains, pairs or tetrads.

Bacilli: rod-shaped cells; some are short, pleomorphic or spore-forming.

Curved/spiral forms: include curved rods and helical organisms.

3. Culture and colony description

Describe colony size, shape, margin, elevation, surface, opacity, pigment and hemolysis when relevant.

Culture appearance alone is not definitive identification. Media and incubation requirements differ among organisms.

4. Biochemical identification

Common examples include catalase, coagulase, oxidase, indole, urease, citrate, carbohydrate reactions and selected enzyme tests.

Not every test applies to every organism. Interpret with positive/negative controls and validated reference criteria.

5. Serology and antigen tests

Serology detects antibodies or antigens, depending on the assay. It is useful for selected infections and surveillance programs.

It is not a universal test for all bacteria; timing, vaccination and exposure history can affect interpretation.

6. Disease pathology

VET

Record the affected system, species/host, age group, clinical pattern and gross or histopathological findings.

Lesions are not always specific to one bacterium and should be linked to laboratory evidence.
